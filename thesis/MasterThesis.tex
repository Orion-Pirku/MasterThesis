\section{Abstract}
\textbf{Ever since its discovery, the role of \textit{prdm9} has been described as detrimental to
	successful propagation of meiotic recombination in sexually reproducing eukaryotes. \textit{Prdm9}'s
	key role is the trimethylating of histone 3 lysine 4 and histone 3 lysine 36, which indicate the
	locations where recombination should take place, thus moving the so called hotspots away from functional genome elements,
	such as genes and promoters. Recently studies have uncovered that not all sexually
	reproducing organisms carry the gene, yet still undergo recombination successfully, most notably \textit{Aves, Crocodilia, Canes, \& Percomorpha}.
	Moreover, it was discovered that \textit{prdm9} independent recombination hotspots occur even in
	species carrying the gene. This finding underscores the important of understanding the underlying
	mechanism of \textit{prdm9} independent recombination. In this study I seek to computationally investigate this
	alternate pathway of recombination by computing the population scaled recombination rate of
	\textit{Sylvia atricapilla} (European blackcap), investigating the recombination landscape and identifying hotspots from recombination rate map.
	To do so I utilize \texttt{pyrho}, a software able to compute demography informed populuation scaled
	recombination rate as well as continuous wavelet transformation which I use to call hotspot peaks
	from local background noise via a peak prominence threshold, ultimately detecting 712 hotspots when
	using a threshold of 90\% percentile of recombination values and 6142 hotspots when using a
	threshold of 30\% percentile of recombination values}

\section{Introduction}
\subsection{Overview of Meiosis}
Meiosis is a specialized cell division process that generates haploid gametes from diploid germ cells in sexually reproducing organisms \parencite{handel_genetics_2010}. Following a single round of DNA replication, two sequential divisions take place, during which homologous chromosomes segregate. A key feature of meiosis is \textbf{meiotic recombination}, which takes place during Prophase I of meiosis and reshuffles alleles between homologous (parental) chromosomes, ultimately enhancing genetic diversity \parencite{hunter_meiotic_2015, baudat_meiotic_2013}. Recombination is a complicated process, involving the induction of programmed DNA double-strand breaks (DSBs), $5'$ stand resection, homologous strand invasion, Holliday junction formation, and resolution into crossover (CO) or non-crossover (NCO) events, including DSBs repair \parencite{lange_landscape_2016, borner_crossovernoncrossover_2004}. These events occur at specific chromosomal loci termed \textbf{recombination hotspots}. Recombination hotspots are essential for meiosis, as at least one recombination event has to occur per chromosome in order for gametogenesis to be successful \parencite{lange_landscape_2016}.

\subsection{Meiotic Recombination Hotspots and \textit{prdm9}}
In most mammals, the location of recombination hotspots is largely determined by \textbf{PRDM9} \parencite{powers_meiotic_2016, imai_prdm9_2017, cavassim_prdm9_2022, abualia_natural_2024}. PRDM9 is a histone methyl--transferase consisting of three domains: 1. The KRAB domain that recruits the recombination machinery; 2. The PR/SET domain that catalyzes the trimethylation of H3K4 and H3K36 hence marking the hotspot locations; 3. The hypervariable $C_2H_2$ zinc finger array that binds to specific DNA motifs \parencite{powers_meiotic_2016, imai_prdm9_2017, cavassim_prdm9_2022}. Once bound to its DNA motifs, PRDM9 trimethylates H3K4 and H3K36; recruits factors such as SPO11, MLH1, DMC1/RAD51, and ZCWPW1/2 to initiate double-strand breaks, promote homologous strand exchange, and ensure DSBs repair \parencite{parvanov_prdm9_2017, tian_ewsr1_2021}. However, with every meiotic event, PRDM9 imposes evolutionary pressure on itself: with each repair of DSBs at its DNA binding sites, \textit{prdm9} motif mutations can accumulate, gradually eroding motifs and forcing a continuous turnover of PRDM9's zinc finger array \parencite{baker_prdm9_2015}.

\subsection{Recombination occurs even in the absence of \textit{prdm9}}
While the presence of the \textit{prdm9} gene is a must in humans, mice, apes etc.; some organisms such as birds undergo meiotic recombination even the absence of \textit{prdm9} \parencite{cavassim_prdm9_2022, pigozzi_chromosomes_2016}. In birds the \textit{prdm9} gene has been lost during evolution, yet their chromosomes undergo meiotic recombination seamlessly \parencite{pigozzi_chromosomes_2016}. Furthermore, a recent study \parencite{joseph_high_2024} showed the prevalence of \textit{prdm9}--independent recombination hotspots even in organisms that carry the \textit{prdm9} gene. These findings raise relevant questions such as: 1. Why do organisms carrying \textit{prdm9} retain \textit{prdm9}--independent recombination hotspots? 2. Do any cross--over/non--crossover events take place in \textit{prdm9}--independent recombination hotspots? 3. What are the underlying recombination mechanisms in \textit{prdm9}--independent hotspots?
4. Are these \textit{prdm9}--independent hotspots evolutionary conserved and if so why?
\subsection{Methods for Studying Meiotic Recombination Hotspots}
The traditional methodology for studying meiotic recombination involves the use of cytological methods, conducted on spermatocytes or oocytes. The methods include immuno--staining of meiotic markers (such as PRDM9, MLH1, SYCP3, SYCP1, HORMAD1/2) as well as Histone Modifications (such as H3K4me3/36) \parencite{lee_ewsr1_2019, parvanov_prdm9_2016, lam_cell-type-specific_2019, hinch_configuration_2020, pigozzi_chromosomes_2016}. While the immuno--staining methods provide a good broad--scale overview of meiosis it also has several drawbacks. They lack of resolution, are unable to quantify the strength of a recombination event, have limited ability to determine the evolutionary history of recombination events across the genome, as well as difficulty to establish a baseline as the recombination landscape if meiosis factors differ in different species, such as the presence/absance of \textit{prdm9} \parencite{pigozzi_chromosomes_2016, baudat_meiotic_2013}. However, there is another angle at which meiotic recombination can be studied. Computational methods utilizing linkage disequilibrium, that is the degree of non--random association between two alleles, are a good alternative for determining the location of recombination events along the genome \parencite{10.1093/molbev/msae112, mcvean_genealogical_2002}.\texttt{LDhat} estimates the composite likelihood of recombination rates being observed at a genomic position by modeling recombination rates under the assumption of constant population sizes; \texttt{LDhot} is a extension of \texttt{LDhat} which determines the presence of recombination hotspots given a recombination rate via a likelihood ratio test and \texttt{LDhelmet} which is a re--implementation of \texttt{LDhat} that makes it possible to analyze highly divergent species \parencite{slatkin_linkage_2008, 10.1093/genetics/160.3.1231, auton_recombination_2007, 10.1371/journal.pgen.1003090}. While these tools yield fine scale resolution of recombination hotspots, they neither account for the complicated demographic history such as bottlenecks, recent expansions or population structure nor are they computationally feasible for large scale population studies \parencite{doi:10.1126/sciadv.aaw9206}. \texttt{Pyrho} solves this issue by taking advantage of two locus sample likelihood and computes recombination rates by maximizing a composite likelihood built from two-SNP haplotype probabilities, where those probabilities are derived under a demography-aware coalescent model, such as one simulated through \texttt{msprime} \parencite{doi:10.1126/sciadv.aaw9206, baumdicker_efficient_2022}.

\subsection{Main objectives}
In this study I seek investigate the fine scale \textit{prdm9}--independent meiotic recombination landscape. To this end, I will extend the analysis of the recombination rate landscape previously conducted on 30 European blackcap (\textit{Sylvia atricapilla}) individuals to a larger dataset of 100 individuals \parencite{bascon-cardozo-fine-scale-2024}. European Blackcaps are a excellent choice of for a model organism as there are extensive genome sequences, demography models and previous studies which have been conducted on blackcaps, albeit at a lower scale \parencite{ishigohoka_high-recombining_2025, bascon-cardozo-fine-scale-2024, bours_blackcap_2023}. The population scaled recombination rate landscape will be estimated through \texttt{pyrho} using previously published demography models via \texttt{msprime} and individual genetic variation data\parencite{ishigohoka_high-recombining_2025, bascon-cardozo-fine-scale-2024}. Following this the distribution of the recombination rate along the landscape of european blackcaps, overlap with genomic functional elements, GC content, genes, intergenic regions ,UTRs, exons, codons, Nucleotide $\pi$, SNP count, tajimas $D$ \parencite{bascon-cardozo-fine-scale-2024, singhal_2015}.Lastly, to identify the most likely recombination hotspots in the blackcap genome, I will use an alternative to the sliding-window approach of \cite{wooldridge_rapid_2023}. Rather than averaging recombination rates over large sliding windows and loosing resolution as well as risk flattening peaks, I will utilize continuous wavelet transformation which circumvent these issues \parencite{Mexican-hat-wavelet} to identify peaks that persist across several genomic windows starting at small 50 bp and going up to 10000 bp.

\section{Materials \& Methods}
\subsection{Data Acquisition}
To computationally identify and characterize prdm9-independent hotspots in
European blackcap (\textit{Sylvia atricapilla}), hereafter referred to as
blackcap, on 15 March 2025 I retrieved:
\begin{itemize}[label=--,itemsep=0pt]
	\item Population level blackcap whole genome variant call \texttt{VCF}, blackcap
	      individual identifiers and inferred demography history data through SMC from
	      \parencite{ishigohoka_high-recombining_2025}.
	\item Blackcap genome assembly, \texttt{GFF} and genome sizes from NCBI with genome
	      accession number \texttt{GCF\_009819655.1} \parencite{NCBI}.
	\item Zebra finch to blackcap lift-over chain file
	      \texttt{taeGut2ToGCF\_009819655.1.over.chain} from the UCSC genome browser
	      \parencite{UCSC, UCSC_2006_LiftOver}.
	\item Zebra finch recombination rate maps from \cite{singhal_2015}
\end{itemize}
As the demography data from \cite{ishigohoka_high-recombining_2025} was computed for each blackcap
sub population, I merged the msmc2 table by taking the average of each input across all tables.
I automated this process with a custom script \texttt{merge\_msmc2\_table.py}.

\subsection{Population Genomics Statistics}
To describe the different aspects of genomic variation, such as average pairwise sequence
variation between individuals, frequency of polymorphic sites and deviation from natural evolution I
computed key population genomics statistics: \textbf{Tajima's D},
\textbf{Window $\pi$} and \textbf{SNP density} using \texttt{VCFTools}
(v0.1.16) \parencite{vcftools, Tajima-1989, nei-and-li-1979}. To compute the statistics I first sampled 100
random individual identifiers using \texttt{GNU shuf} and saved them into a
file called \texttt{n100\_blackcap.panel}. Using this file, I then extracted
the corresponding SNP records with a minor allele frequency \texttt{maf} of
0.05\% and computed the statistics in window sizes of 1000, 5000, 10,000 and
100,000 base pairs (bp). Next, I plotted the genome-wide distribution of each
statistic against their midpoints, which where calculated through the formula:
$\frac{Start + End}{2}$ \\ Lastly, divided the statistics into three equal
quantile-based bins representing "low", "medium" and "high" values and
visualized the proportion of each quantity per chromosome. I automated the
visualization with a custom script called \texttt{plot\_popgen\_stats.py}.

\subsection{Inference of Recombination Rate Maps}
To compute the population scaled recombination rate maps in blackcaps, I
utilized a demography- aware method to infer fine-scale recombination rates
between SNPs called \texttt{pyrho} \parencite{doi:10.1126/sciadv.aaw9206}. I
first precomputed the likelihood tables using \texttt{pyrho make\_table} needed
for estimating the population-specific recombination rate
\parencite{doi:10.1126/sciadv.aaw9206}. The table was precomputed with the
following parameters:
\begin{itemize}[label=--, itemsep=0pt]
	\item Mutation rate $\mu: 4.6\times10^{-9}$, as reported in
	      \cite{bascon-cardozo-fine-scale-2024}
	\item Decimal relative tolerance: 0.1 as reported in
	      \cite{bascon-cardozo-fine-scale-2024}
	\item Population size (i.e., number of haplotypes): 200
	\item Moran population size: 200
	\item The averaged MSMC2 demography history data.
\end{itemize}
I automated this process with a custom script, \texttt{make\_table.sh}. Secondly, I optimized
the \texttt{block penalty} and \texttt{window size}, two hyper\-parameters' required for
the computation of the population\-scaled recombination rate, using \texttt{pyrho hyperparam}
\parencite{doi:10.1126/sciadv.aaw9206}. I ran 10 simulations over the following grid of values:
\begin{itemize}[label=--, itemsep=0pt]
	\item \textbf{Block Penalty}: 10, 20, 30, 40, 50
	\item \textbf{Window Size}: 25, 30, 50, 100, 1000
\end{itemize}
The optimization used the Averaged MSMC2 demography history and precomputed likelihood table
from the previous step. This step was automated using \texttt{optimize\_hyperparameters.sh}.
\texttt{pyrho hyperparam} returns a table with the pearson and spearman correlation coefficients
and the $L2$ norm. I selected a \textbf{window size of 100} with a \textbf{block penalty of 20} as they maximized
the pearson and spearman correlation coefficient while minimizing the $L2$ norm,
as specified by \parencite{doi:10.1126/sciadv.aaw9206}. I sampled the same 100 individuals from
the \texttt{VCF} data as in section 2.2, but only with allele count of at least 2.
Lastly, using the precomputed likelihood, sampled individual SNP data table, window size of 100
with a block penalty of 20 I computed population\-scaled the recombination rate with
\texttt{pyrho optimize} \parencite{doi:10.1126/sciadv.aaw9206}.
This process was automated with a custom script called \texttt{pyrho\_optimize.sh}. The sex
chromosomes Z and W were omitted from the analysis because their recombination patterns are
irregular and not comparable to the autosomes \parencite{pigozzi_chromosomes_2016}.
\subsection{Analysis of Recombination Rate Landscape Over The Blackcap Genome}

To investigate the population scaled recombination rate landscape in blackcaps, I
converted the raw recombination rate to \texttt{cM/Mb} by averaging over
genomic windows of 10 kilobases and multiplying by $10^8$. Then, I calculated
the midpoint of the genomic coordinates by the formula: $\frac{Start +End}{2}$.
Lastly, I plotted the recombination rate in \texttt{cM/Mb} over the
genomic midpoint coordinates. I investigated the distribution of
recombination rate strength per chromosome. To do this, I sorted the
recombination rate per chromosome by signal strength and divided it into equal
tertiles ("low", "medium" and "high"). I visualized the data on a chromosomal
basis using pie charts. \\
To gain a more in\-depth understanding of the recombination landscape I
incorporated the gene density and gc content into the analysis. I extracted the
genes from the blackcap \texttt{gff} file using a custom script
\texttt{clean\_raw\_gff.py} and computed the gene density over genomic windows and divided
by 1 Mb to get $\frac{genes}{Mb}$. I generated genomic bins of size $100000$ using \texttt{PyRanges},
counted the number of genes overlapping with each window and divided by the
window size and plotted the distribution of the gene density along the genome
and binned the gene density into tertiles representing "low", "medium" and
"high"
.To compute the gc content, I used \texttt{bedtools makewindows} to make genomic windows
per each chromosome, \texttt{bedtools nuc} to compute the gc content over the windows,
which I automated with the script \texttt{compute\_gc\_content.sh}.
Next, I plotted the gc content over each
chromosome and binned it into three tertiles ("low", "medium" and "high")
values to visualize how the gc content is distributed over the genome. I
automated the plotting in the steps above using the script
\texttt{plot\_popgen\_stats.py} \\

To quantify the correlation between the population genomic statistics, gc
content, gene density and the recombination rate I first intersected each
feature with one another using \texttt{pyRanges}, computed the pearson
correlation coefficient between each genomic feature using \texttt{SciPy} and
visualized the results in a heatmap \parencite{2020SciPy-NMeth}.I automated
this analysis with a custom script called
\texttt{compute\_feature\_correlation.py}. \\

Lastly, I investigated the average profile of the recombination rate over
genomic features (UTR's, CDS, Exons, Intergenic Regions and Genes), which I
extracted from the blackcap \texttt{GFF} using the \texttt{clean\_raw\_gff.py}.
I used deeptool's \texttt{computeMatrix} to calculate the average profile of
the recombination rate over these regions and \texttt{plotHeatmap} to plot the
average profile and heatmap. The average recombination rate profile was
computed for $\pm$ 20 kb and zeros were not reported. The script for computing
the matrix and plotting the profile are:
\texttt{computeRhoMatrix.sh} and
\texttt{plotHeatmap.sh}, respectively.

\subsection{Inference of Meiotic Recombination Hotspots}
To call recombination hotspots from recombination rate maps, I applied
\textbf{Continious Wavelet Transform (CWT)} to detect multi-scale peaks in the
signal. To do so the wavelet is continiously rescaled accross a grid of
specified scales and shifted along the signal, producing coefficients that
quantify local similarity between the signal and the wavelet shape at each
position and scale. The higher the similarity, the higher coefficients
\parencite{Singh2022, Mexican-hat-wavelet}. Before computing the cwt with
\texttt{PyWavelet}, I smoothed the recombination rate $\rho$ signal using
\texttt{SciPy's} one dimensional gaussian filter (\texttt{gaussian_filter1d})
($\sigma = 5$) to remove excessive high frequency noise. Then, I generated 200
logarithmically spaced scales betweem 50 and 600. With an recombination rate
genomic range of 22 bp this corresponds to genomic bin size between $\sim$ 1.1
kb to $\sim$ 13.2 kb. After computing the wavelet coefficients, I normalized
the coefficients at each scale independently through \texttt{z-score}.
Following this, I selected only those coefficients, which had a z-score: $z >
	2.0$ and persisted in atleast 30\% of the scales. The peaks were then
identified using SciPy's \texttt{find_peaks} with a minimum peak prominence of
at least the $90^{th}$ percentile. This process was automated using the script
\texttt{find_hotspots.py}. Lastly, I used the package \texttt{cobind.py} to
quantify the amount of overlap between the identified recombination hotspots
and genomic features (UTRs', codons, genes, intergenic regions) using the npmi metric which gives
the enrichment of overlaps relative to what is expected at random \parencite{ma_cobind_2023} \\
To test if the wavelet transform methodology is
also applicable to other prdm9 independent recombining species I ran the same
analysis on \texttt{LDHelmet} recombination maps generated by \cite{singhal_2015}.

\subsection{Wavelet transformation identifies meiotic recombination hotspots in blackcaps}
To identify sharp peaks in the recombination rate, I applied a continious wavelet transformation.
Continious wavelet transformations capture sharp, multi-scale differences in genomic signal
intensity, making them suitable for detecting jumps in the recombination rate
\parencite{Mexican-hat-wavelet, Lee2019}. \\
Utilizing wavelet transform to identify the ridges of the recombination rate signal with a peak
prominence threshold of 90 and 30, I identified 712, and 6145 putative recombination hotspots
(See Materials \& Methods for details). The putative hotspots identified with a threshold of 90,
are located mostly in the subtelomeric regions of chromosomes. Hotspots also appear along
the chromosomal body, especially in the smaller chromosomes (Figure 8, Supplementary Figure 8-9). The same trend is apparent in
putative hotspots identified with a threshold of 30, although hotspots are also observed in the
chromosomal body of the larger chromosomes, suggesting a broader distribution at lower detection
thresholds (Figure 8, Supplementary Figures 8-9). \\

\section{Discussion}
\subsection{Recent population expansion and high diversity in the blackcap genome}
When conducting a population genomics study, especially in non-model organisms such as \textit{European blackcaps},
it is important to investigate general statistics such as tajima's $D$, nucleotide diversity
$\pi$ and SNP density \parencite{Tajima-1989, nei-and-li-1979}. These measures yield important
information about the genome. Firstly, Nucleotide diversity $\pi$ measures the average pairwise differences per site
between chromosomes, which reflects the amount of variation retained withing a genomic region
\parencite{nielsen2013introduction, nei-and-li-1979}. High $\pi$ is an indicator of effective
breaks in the linkage between sites, which is a result of high recombination. Conversly, low $\pi$
is expected in low recombining regions where background selection or hitchhiking are more prominent.
The blackcap population has a genome wide nucleotide $\pi$ of 0.0056, which is 0.44 percentage points
higher than in humans ($\pi = 0.0011$) (Table 1) \parencite{Li-and-Sadler-1991}. Furthermore, the
$\pi$ values were higher in the microchromosomes, with chromosome 33 having the highest value of
0.0088 (Table 1). These high $\pi$ values imply high recombination activity in the
genomes of blackcaps. \\
Secondly, Tajima's $D$, another statistic in population genomics, measures the difference between the
nuleotide $\pi$ and the number of segregating sites $\theta_{w}$, mirroring the shape of the allele
frequency spectrum \parencite{nielsen2013introduction, Tajima-1989}. A negative $D$ is a indicator
of excess low frequency alleles, that arise from recent selective sweeps, reduced recombination or
recent population expansions \parencite{nielsen2013introduction, Tajima-1989}. Values near zero
reflect neutral equilibrium while positive values result from balancing selection. The blackcap
genome has an average tajima's $D$ of -1.0572, which remains relatively stable accross chromosomes
(Table 1). Local negative tajima's $D$ values could indicate selective sweeps or reduced
recombination, however since the tajima'S $D$ doesnt show extreme drops accross the chromosomes and
retains a negative value it indicates a recent population expansion event or low frequency alleles
that are continiously generated and removed via background selection and/or recombination events (Figure 1, Supplementary
Figure 1).\\
Thirdly, the SNP density represents the number of polymorphic sites per genomic window
\parencite{nielsen2013introduction}. Regions with high SNP Density reflect high recombination or
weaker purifying selection, and vice versa. The blackcap genome has $\approx 54 \frac{Variants}{kb}$;
the microchromosomes having a higher SNP density than macrochromosomes (Table 1, Figure 2). As high
SNP density is indicative of standing variation. \\
Lastly, incorporating these three measurements together one can suggest that the blackcaps are a
large, high recombining population that maintain their high genetic diversity with a
recent demographic expansion.

\subsection{Strong correlation between gc content and recombination rate in blackcaps}
The GC content has been repeatedly shown to have a strong correlation with the recombination rate in
organisms from yeast to humans \parencite{kiktev2018, Chen2006, Fullerton2001}. To check if this is
also the case in blackcaps I computed the gc content in 100 kb windows and computed the pearson
correlation coefficient (Figure 6). The resuting value of $r \approx 0.55$ is consistent with what
has been reported before in humans, mice and yeasts. Mainly strong correlation between gc content
and recombination rate \parencite{kiktev2018, Chen2006, Fullerton2001}. There are several
possibilities that could explain this correlation. \\
Firstly, during the repair of DNA double stranded breaks needed to form recombination hotspots a
bias towards preferential DSB repair with GC over AT nucleotides could arise, which is known as GC
Biased Gene Conversion \parencite{Bolivar2019,
	DuretGaltier2009}. Secondly, blackcaps could have a mutation bias towards GC nucleotides. Such
events where organisms have preferential dinucleotides to which their genome mutates over time have
been observed in both prokaryotes \parencite{Hershberg2010}. Thirdly, the high GC content on the
blackcap genome could be due to selection for stable DNA. GC pairs have 3 hydrogen bonds as opposed
to 2 between AT pairs, thus rendering them more stable towards excessive double stranded breaks as
birds do not have the \textit{prdm9} gene, which also plays a role in controlling excessive DNA
breaks during recombination \parencite{paiano_atm_2020, MustonenLaessig2005}. Lastly, the high GC
Content could be attributed to high gene density and functional elements \parencite{Bird1986}, which
is supported by a correlation coefficient of $r \approx 0.7$ (Figure 6)

\subsection{Blackcap 5' UTRs are enriched in recombination hotspots}
In organisms carrying \textit{prdm9}, recombination is directed away from functional genomic
elements, by the trimethylation of histones 3 lysine 4 (H3K4me3) and histone 3 lysine 36 (H3K36me3)
\parencite{cavassim_prdm9_2022, parvanov_prdm9_2017, lange_landscape_2016, baker_prdm9_2015, berg_prdm9_2010}.
However birds lack the \textit{prdm9} gene completely \parencite{cavassim_prdm9_2022}.
To investigate if the absence of \textit{prdm9} leads to enriched recombination rate in functional
genomic features I computed the enrichment and average profile of the recombination rate over genes,
UTR's, CDS, exons and intergenic regions. The recombination rate had a sharp prominent peak in the
center of 5' UTRs ($\rho \approx 2.5^10{-7}$), followed by small peaks on cds and exons while
showing weak or no signal intergenic regions and the center of genes (Figure 7).\\
An important observation is that the recombination rate is elevated in subtelomeric regions, with some peaks
along the chromosomal axis, characteristics which have been previously described in zebra finches
and long tailed finches, pointing towards possible evolutionary conservation of recombination
hotspots \parencite{singhal_2015}. However this hypothesis could not be tested due to the absence
lift over files needed to map the genomic intervals of finches to the genome blackcaps caused by
lack of funding in the team of \texttt{UCSC} Genome Browser needed to generate such files. \\

Recombination maps, computed by pyrho, convey information about all historic recombination
activity across the genome, because the calculation incorporates demographic history information to infer recombination
rates \parencite{doi:10.1126/sciadv.aaw9206}. As recombination activity is turned over with time,
the most recent recombination hotspots are expected to have higher recombination rates than older
hotspots\parencite{UbedaWilkins2011}. So one needs to identify large jumps in recombination rate
values over a local background. Previously recombination hotspots were called from
recombination maps inferred through LD methods with a sliding window approach \parencite{wooldridge_rapid_2023}.
However, the sliding window approach lacks multi scale resolution and risks flattening peaks as it
computes an average over a large window, loosing fine scale resolution. To circumvent these
limitations I applied the continuous wavelet transformation \parencite{Mexican-hat-wavelet}. \\

The continuous wavelet transformation consists of a "mother" wavelet function that is slid
across a signal over multiple scales and computes how well a signal fits the wavelet at that
position and scale. After computing the wavelet coefficient, I retained  the coefficients that persisted over at least 30\% of the scales.
Next I applied another level of thresholding by computing the per scale Z-Score and selecting only those coefficients with A $Z >
	2.0$. Finally, the coefficients representing the same peak over several scales are collapsed into a
single coefficient, that is used to call identify peaks based on its prominence over neighbouring
coefficients. \\

I was able to identify 712 putative hotspots when using a
peak prominence threshold equal to the 90th percentile of recombination rate values and 6145 when
using a threshold of 30. When computing the normalized pointwise mutual information, it was clearly
seen that hotspots followed the trend of having the highest enrichment in 5' UTRs as opposed to
other genomic features, although this signal was more prominent in the hotspots identified with a
threshold of 30 (Table 2). This jump in NPMI in the lower threshold of 30, which is observed between
hotspots and 5' UTRs could be an artifact of the mathematics behind normalized mutual pointwise
information, which when comparing the quantifying the enrichment of only a few intervals (712 windows) within a
larger quantity of windows such as 5' UTRs (42816 windows) might underestimate the true value\\

To test if this methodology is also applicable to other datasets which were not generated through
pyrho, I utilized the recombination rate maps from \cite{singhal_2015} that were generated through
\texttt{LDhot}. In this case the wavelet coefficients still identified recombination hotspots which
were mostly localized in the subtelomeric regions with occasional ones along the chromosomal axis
(Figure 9, Supplementary Figure 10)

\subsection{Conclusions}
This thesis seeked to extend the analysis of the meiotic recombination landscape of blackcap
populations from 30 individuals \parencite{bascon-cardozo-fine-scale-2024} to 100 as well as
establish a novel, accurate methodology for calling present recombination hotspots from historic
recombination rate data. Using population genome statistics such as Tajima's $D$,
nucleotide $\pi$ and snp density; Blackcap \texttt{GFF} files (containing location of genes,
UTRs, exons and codons), computing the GC content and gene density as well the recombination rate
with pyrho, I was able to uncover that the blackcap genome although it shows recent population expansions
still has relatively high diversity, high gc content and high recombination activity (Table 1). Futhermore
these features showed extreme values at subtelomeric regions (Figures 1 - 5, Supplementary Figures
1-7), with the recombination rate, gene density, gc content, $\pi$ and gene density increasing in the microchromosomes.
Recombination rate achieved the highest average enrichment over 5' UTRs ($\rho = 2.5 \times 10^{-7}$),
with small peaks in codons and exons ($\rho \approx 1.2 \times 10^{-7}$) while no recombination rate
was found in intragenic regions as it has been observed in species carrying \textit{prdm9}
\parencite{wooldridge_rapid_2023, lange_landscape_2016, baker_prdm9_2015}. Furthermore the presence
of strong recombination rate signal in the subtelomeric regions mirrored findings in zebra finches
and long tailed finches \parencite{singhal_2015}. Finally wavelet transformation provides a novel,
more accurate and computationally efficient methodology to identify hotspots from recombination rate
maps, as they are able to analyze the signal at a multi-scale level thus yielding fine scale
resolution and robust noise handling (Figure 8) as well as being persistent in datasets generated by
LD methods (Figure 9)(\parencite{singhal_2015}).
